It had come out of the that summer, after three long meetings with the registrar, the deans, and a vice president who kept asking why the answer couldn't simply be zero. The grading portal, everyone finally agreed, could be down for four hours before the damage turned serious, and it could . They had all signed it. Lea had taped the signature page beside the number.

South Bridgee had come a long way since March. The backups were locked now — , refusing every delete command, including the ones Lea sent herself just to watch them fail. The still ran every week. If the question was “do we still have our data?”, the answer had never been more certain.

Nobody had asked the other question yet: how long would it take to get it back?

The typhoon arrived on the Sunday night before final grades were due. Power across the city flickered for most of the evening. At 2:40 a.m., the building's generator kicked in, kicked out, and kicked in again — and somewhere in that third surge, the under the grading portal's database server stopped answering. It did not catch fire. It did not make a sound. It simply stopped, and it took the live database with it.

Lea got the call at 6:10 a.m. By 6:45 she had confirmed the array was not coming back, and at 7:02 a.m. — she wrote the time down, because the BIA said to — she . The clock on the whiteboard had started. Four hours meant 11:02.

The steps still mostly made sense, the way directions to a house still make sense after the street has been renamed. Step 4 pointed to a that no longer existed. Paolo did the math on a sticky note at 8:15 and didn't say the answer out loud. He just held it up: about seven hours.

They improvised, the way people do with a clock running. The , on a storage box at the far end of the server room, had survived the surge, so they restored from that instead — a route that was nowhere in the runbook and had never been tried on the new server. At 12:51 p.m. — nearly two hours past the line on the whiteboard — the portal's login page loaded. Paolo actually cheered. A dean who had been standing in the doorway for most of an hour sent a message to every faculty member: The portal is back. Please resume grade submission.

At 1:20 p.m., the first professor called. Her grades were there — but only up to Saturday night. Everything she had encoded on Sunday was gone. The 1:00 a.m. backup on Monday had started during the power flickers and never finished, and had gone to a mailbox nobody read at night; the newest complete snapshot was from 1:00 a.m. on Sunday, more than a full day before the failure. At 1:34 p.m., a second professor reported that he could log in but could not open any of his classes. At 2:05 p.m., a third noticed something worse: three of her students' final grades showed values she was certain she had never entered. The data was correct. The rebuilt configuration was not — it had loaded last year's grading scale.

The portal was not truly recovered until Tuesday afternoon. In the , Lea wrote three new lines on the whiteboard, under the green one. Planned: 4 hours. Login page: 5 hours, 49 minutes. Actually working, with correct grades: 31 hours.

She looked at them for a long time. She had spent a year making sure the data would survive, and the data had survived. Cybersecurity Dictionary

Backup set (backup scope)

The list of files, folders and systems a backup actually includes. Anything outside it is not protected.

In the storyThe config file, certificate and roster settings were "never in the backup set at all."Think of it likeLike a moving checklist. If the box was never on the list, it does not arrive at the new house.

Bandwidth (the offsite link)

How much data a network connection can move per second. It decides how long a big restore takes.

In the storyThe offsite link was sized for small nightly uploads, not a 312 GB restore. Paolo's math: about seven hours.Think of it likeLike a narrow road. Fine for a tricycle every night, but a ten-wheeler truck crawls.

Business Impact Analysis (BIA)

A study of how much harm the organization suffers, hour by hour, when a system is down or data is lost.

In the storyThe three long meetings with the registrar, the deans and a vice president were the BIA. It produced the two numbers on Lea's whiteboard.Think of it likeLike asking your family, "If the water goes out, how many hours before it becomes a real problem?" The answer decides how fast you must fix it.

Configuration file

A file of settings that tells software how to run: which database, which grading scale, which addresses.

In the storyThe portal's configuration was never backed up. The rebuilt one loaded last year's grading scale, so correct data showed wrong grades.Think of it likeLike your phone's settings. Restore your photos onto a new phone without the settings and things still look "off."

Data classification

Labeling data by how important or sensitive it is, so it gets the right protection, including backups.

In the storyThe missing files sat "in a folder nobody had classified," so nobody thought to protect them.Think of it likeLike sorting documents into "keep forever," "keep for a year" and "trash." Unsorted papers get lost.

Declaring a disaster

The formal decision that an outage is a disaster, which starts the recovery plan and the RTO clock.

In the storyLea declared a disaster at 7:02 a.m. and wrote the time down, so the four-hour limit ran to 11:02.Think of it likeLike a school officially suspending classes. Once announced, everyone follows the emergency plan.

Hash

A short digital fingerprint calculated from a file. If even one bit changes, the hash changes.

In the story"Its hash matched" proved the restored database was identical to what was backed up.Think of it likeLike a fingerprint. Two different files will not have the same one.

Immutable backup

A backup that cannot be changed or deleted, by anyone, until its lock period ends.

In the storySouth Bridgee's backups were "locked now — immutable for thirty-five days," so even Lea's own delete commands failed.Think of it likeLike writing in pen in a logbook that is sealed for 35 days. No eraser, no tearing out pages.

Incremental backup

A backup that sends only what changed since the last backup. Fast every night, but a full restore still moves everything.

In the storyNightly backups "only sent what had changed," so nobody noticed the link could not carry a full restore.Think of it likeLike uploading only today's new photos to the cloud instead of your whole gallery.

Integrity check

A test that proves stored data has not been changed or corrupted, usually by comparing hashes.

In the storyThe weekly integrity checks proved the data still existed and was intact. They did not prove it could be restored on time.Think of it likeLike counting the money in an envelope before and after it is passed around.

Local repository

A backup copy kept on-site, close to the servers. Fastest to restore from, but exposed to the same local disasters.

In the storyThe local storage box survived, so the team restored from it, a route never tried before.Think of it likeLike a spare key in your drawer. Quicker than calling the locksmith, if the house is still standing.

Monitoring alert

An automatic warning that something failed. It is only useful if a person sees it and acts.

In the storyThe alert about the failed Monday backup went to a mailbox nobody read at night.Think of it likeLike a smoke alarm in an empty house. It rings, but nobody hears it.

Offsite repository

A backup copy stored in a different location, so a fire, flood or typhoon at the main site cannot destroy it.

In the storyThe 312 GB database was safe in the offsite repository, but pulling it back would take about seven hours.Think of it likeLike keeping copies of your important IDs at a relative's house in another town.

Post-incident review

A blameless meeting after an incident to record what happened, why, and what to change.

In the storyIn the review, Lea wrote the real numbers on the whiteboard: planned 4 hours, actually working 31 hours.Think of it likeLike a team watching the game video after a loss to fix the plays.

Recovery Point Objective (RPO)

The most data, measured in time, the organization can afford to lose. It decides how often backups must run.

In the story"Lose no more than one day of data" is the portal's RPO. A failed Monday backup meant Sunday's grades fell outside it.Think of it likeLike a game's auto-save. If it saves every hour, the worst you lose is one hour of progress.

Recovery runbook

Step-by-step instructions for restoring a system. It only works if it matches the system you actually run.

In the storyThe eleven-page runbook was neat but written for a server retired in January, so several steps pointed to things that no longer existed.Think of it likeLike a recipe written for your old kitchen. The steps make sense, but the oven it mentions is gone.

Recovery test (rehearsal)

Practicing a restore on a schedule, against the clock, to find the gaps before a real disaster does.

In the story"The recovery had never been rehearsed." A drill would have exposed the old runbook, the missing files and the slow link.Think of it likeLike a fire drill. You find the blocked exit during practice, not during the fire.

Recovery Time Objective (RTO)

The longest a service may stay down before the damage becomes unacceptable. It is a time limit for getting the service back.

In the story"RTO 4 HRS" on the whiteboard: the grading portal had to be working again within four hours of the disaster being declared.Think of it likeLike a store's rule that the cashier system must be back within 30 minutes, or customers walk out.

Service validation

Testing that a restored system actually works correctly for real users, not just that it starts up.

In the storyThe login page loaded at 12:51 p.m., but grades were missing and wrong. The service answered; it was not yet right.Think of it likeLike checking that a repaired car can brake and steer, not just that the engine starts.

Snapshot

A saved copy of data exactly as it was at one moment in time.

In the storyThe database's locked snapshot was exactly where it should be, so the data itself was safe.Think of it likeLike a photo of your whiteboard notes. It shows exactly what was written at that moment.

Standby virtual machine

A spare virtual server kept ready to take over when the main one fails.

In the storyStep 4 of the runbook pointed to a standby VM that had been deleted.Think of it likeLike a spare tire. Useful only if it is actually in the trunk.

Storage controller / storage array

The hardware that manages a group of disks (the array). If the controller fails, the disks behind it can become unreachable.

In the storyA power surge stopped the storage controller under the database server, and the live database went with it.Think of it likeLike a building's elevator controller. The floors are still there, but nobody can reach them.

TLS certificate

A digital ID that lets a website prove who it is and encrypt traffic (the padlock and https:// in your browser).

In the storyThe portal's certificate lived only on the old server, so the team had to request a new one during the outage.Think of it likeLike the padlock icon when you log in to your bank. Without it, browsers warn you the site is not safe.
